WHY FIRE RISK ASSESSMENT?

Fire risk assessment is not optional. It is a legal duty placed on every person who has control of non-domestic premises across all four nations of the United Kingdom. For holiday parks and hospitality premises, the requirements are particularly significant because you are responsible for people who are sleeping in an unfamiliar environment. This guide explains the law, the regional variances, the local council requirements, and the specific challenges that make holiday parks a unique fire safety environment.

THE LEGAL DUTY — WHO MUST COMPLY?

Across the entire United Kingdom, fire safety legislation places a mandatory duty on specific individuals to carry out fire risk assessments. These individuals are known by different titles depending on which nation's legislation applies, but the duty itself is universal: if you have control of non-domestic premises, you must assess the risks from fire and take appropriate measures.

Critical — This Is Not Guidance, It Is Law Fire risk assessment is a statutory duty, not a recommendation. Failure to carry out an FRA, or failure to act on its findings, is a criminal offence in every part of the United Kingdom.

The person on whom this duty falls is defined as:

  • Scotland: The "dutyholder" — any person who has control to any extent of relevant premises (Fire (Scotland) Act 2005, Sections 53 & 54)
  • England & Wales: The "responsible person" — the employer, owner, or any person who has control of the premises (Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, Article 3)
  • Northern Ireland: The "appropriate person" — the employer or any person who has control of relevant premises (Fire and Rescue Services (Northern Ireland) Order 2006, Article 25 & 26)

For holiday parks, this will typically be the site owner, the operating company, and/or the park manager. Where multiple parties share control — for example, a management company operating on behalf of a landowner — multiple parties may hold concurrent fire safety duties.

Holiday Parks — Dual Duty Holiday parks present a dual assessment scenario. The site itself is relevant premises requiring an FRA covering communal areas, roads, fire points, and site infrastructure. Individual letting units (lodges, static caravans, pods) that are let out as holiday accommodation are also relevant premises requiring their own consideration within the overall assessment.

UK LEGISLATION — FOUR NATIONS, FOUR FRAMEWORKS

Fire safety legislation is devolved. Each of the four UK nations operates under its own primary legislation and secondary regulations. While the underlying principles are broadly similar — identify hazards, assess risks, implement measures, record findings — the specific legal requirements, terminology, guidance documents, and enforcement structures differ significantly.

A fire risk assessment conducted under English guidance may not satisfy Scottish requirements, and vice versa. The Scottish Government and DCLG/Home Office publish separate guidance documents with different chapter structures, different benchmark standards, and different terminology.

WHAT CHANGED AFTER GRENFELL?

The Grenfell Tower fire of 14 June 2017 fundamentally changed fire safety law in England and Wales. While the legislative response was primarily focused on high-rise residential buildings, several amendments have wider implications for all responsible persons, including holiday park operators.

Fire Safety Act 2021 (England & Wales) — commenced 16 May 2022. Clarified that the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 applies to the structure, external walls (including cladding, balconies, and windows), and individual flat entrance doors opening into common parts of multi-occupied residential buildings.

Fire Safety (England) Regulations 2022 — came into force 23 January 2023. Introduced specific new duties for responsible persons of multi-occupied residential buildings, including provision of fire safety instructions to residents, information about fire doors, building plans to fire services, and regular checks on firefighting equipment.

Building Safety Act 2022, Section 156 — came into force 1 October 2023. Made critical amendments to the Fire Safety Order affecting all regulated premises:

Universal Change from 1 October 2023 (England) All responsible persons must now record all findings from their fire risk assessment — not just significant findings. Previously, recording was only required where 5+ employees were present, or premises were licensed, or an Alterations Notice had been served. This threshold has been removed. Every regulated premises in England must now have a fully recorded FRA. The responsible person's name and a UK address must also be recorded in the FRA.

Impact on Scotland: Scotland's legislation already required recording of significant findings where 5+ employees are employed or the premises are subject to licensing or registration. The Scottish Government has not replicated the universal recording requirement introduced in England, but given that most holiday parks fall within the licensing/registration categories, the practical difference for the holiday park sector is minimal.

Impact on Northern Ireland: Northern Ireland's legislation mirrors the Scottish approach. The Fire and Rescue Services (NI) Order 2006 requires recording in similar circumstances. Post-Grenfell amendments have not been applied to the NI framework at the same pace as England.

REGIONAL VARIANCES — KEY DIFFERENCES

While the core duty — assess fire risks, implement measures, keep records — is consistent across the UK, the detail varies in ways that matter operationally. The following highlights the key differences that holiday park operators must understand.

🏴󠁧󠁢󠁳󠁣󠁴󠁿 SCOTLAND

  • Single national enforcing authority: SFRS
  • Dedicated Annex 3 guidance for holiday camping and caravan sites
  • Caravan site licensing under Caravan Sites and Control of Development Act 1960 — fire safety now enforced via Fire (Scotland) Act rather than through licence conditions
  • Recording required where 5+ employees or premises licensed/registered
  • Scottish Government recommends UKAS-accredited assessors or Professional Registration Scheme members
  • BS 5839 Part 1 Category L2 recommended for most sleeping accommodation
  • Short-term let licensing (Civic Government (Scotland) Act 1982) since October 2022 requires fire risk assessment as a condition of STL licence

🏴󠁧󠁢󠁥󠁮󠁧󠁿 ENGLAND

  • 44 local Fire & Rescue Authorities enforce the FSO
  • Model Standards 2008 for Caravan Sites in England (residential) — separate 1989 model standards still referenced for holiday sites
  • Since October 2023: all FRA findings must be recorded (not just significant findings)
  • Responsible person's name and UK address must appear in FRA
  • Higher fines: unlimited fines for certain offences post-Building Safety Act
  • BAFE SP205 scheme for competent fire risk assessors
  • Home Office published new simplified guides (2023) for small sleeping accommodation

🏴󠁧󠁢󠁷󠁬󠁳󠁿 WALES

  • RRO 2005 applies (shared with England) but enforced by 3 Welsh FRS
  • All Wales Self-Catering Holiday Accommodation Guidance — standardised advice across all three FRS areas
  • Mobile Homes (Wales) Act 2013 for residential sites (not holiday)
  • Holiday sites still licensed under 1960 Act
  • Visitor Accommodation Registration from autumn 2026 (Visitor Accommodation (Register and Levy) Etc. (Wales) Act 2025)
  • New visitor levy powers for councils from April 2027
  • Council tax premiums up to 150% on second homes in high-demand areas
  • Self-catering must be available 252 days/year, let 182 days to qualify for business rates

NORTHERN IRELAND

  • Single enforcing authority: NIFRS
  • Legislation came into effect 15 November 2010 — newest of the four frameworks
  • Requirements closely mirror Scotland's approach
  • NIFRS provides premises-specific fire safety guides
  • The "appropriate person" terminology is unique to NI
  • Post-Grenfell changes have not been applied at the same pace as England/Wales
  • RQIA (for regulated accommodation) recommends UKAS-accredited TPCB assessors

LOCAL COUNCIL LICENSING — CARAVAN SITE LICENCES

Beyond the fire safety legislation itself, holiday parks must hold a caravan site licence issued by their local authority under the Caravan Sites and Control of Development Act 1960. This licence typically includes conditions relating to fire safety, and these conditions can vary between councils.

Critical Interaction Between Licensing and Fire Safety Regimes In England and Wales, where the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 applies to the site, the fire authority (not the local council) is responsible for enforcing fire safety. This means the local council cannot duplicate fire safety conditions through the site licence for matters already covered by the RRO. However, the council can — and routinely does — require the site licence applicant to provide a copy of their current fire risk assessment as part of the licensing process. In Scotland, fire safety was similarly removed from the caravan site licensing regime and is now enforced exclusively through the Fire (Scotland) Act 2005 by SFRS.

What Varies Between Councils:

  • Licence conditions: Based on "Model Standards" published by central government, but councils have discretion to vary conditions based on local circumstances. Conditions for holiday sites differ from residential sites.
  • Occupancy periods: Determined by planning permission — typically 8-10 months for holiday sites, but varies by council area. Some Scottish councils permit 11-month seasons.
  • Separation distances: Model Standards specify minimum 5m (aluminium) or 6m (plywood/mixed) between units, but some councils apply stricter requirements based on local fire authority advice.
  • Fire point provision: Model Standards require fire points within 30m of any unit, but councils may impose additional requirements for larger sites or sites with limited water supply.
  • Inspection regimes: Frequency and scope of council site inspections varies significantly. Some councils inspect annually; others only on complaint or change of licence.
  • Density limits: The 1989 Model Standards specify a maximum of 60 caravans per hectare for holiday sites. Some councils apply lower limits.

Scotland-Specific: The Civic Government (Scotland) Act 1982 (Licensing of Short-term Lets) Order 2022 introduced mandatory licensing for short-term lets from 1 October 2022. Individual letting units on holiday parks that are marketed as self-catering accommodation may fall within the scope of this licensing regime. The STL licence application requires confirmation that a fire risk assessment is in place.

Wales-Specific: The Development of Tourism and Regulation of Visitor Accommodation (Wales) Bill will introduce a new licensing framework from 2026/27. This will require providers of self-contained visitor accommodation to hold a licence and demonstrate compliance with "fitness for visitor accommodation" standards, including fire risk assessment, gas/electrical safety certificates, carbon monoxide alarms, and public liability insurance.

HOLIDAY PARK SPECIFIC REQUIREMENTS

Holiday parks present a unique fire safety environment that differs significantly from hotels, offices, or other non-domestic premises. The Scottish Government's Annex 3 and the English Model Standards provide specific guidance, but the following factors make holiday parks a distinct fire risk assessment challenge:

SLEEPING ACCOMMODATION

Guests are sleeping in an unfamiliar environment. They do not know the layout, the escape routes, or the fire safety systems. They may have consumed alcohol. They may be unfamiliar with LPG appliances. Children and elderly visitors may be present. The Scottish guidance explicitly identifies "short-term occupiers of premises where there is no day-to-day management control or supervision" as a specific risk category.

SEPARATION DISTANCES

Fire can spread rapidly between caravans and lodges. Minimum separation of 6m between units (5m for aluminium-clad units with fire-resistant properties). Porches may protrude 1m into the gap. Awnings must maintain 3m clearance to adjacent units. Eaves and bay windows may reduce the gap to a minimum of 5.25m between extremities. One car maximum between adjoining units. No combustible storage beneath or between units.

FIRE POINTS

No unit or site building should be more than 30m from a fire point. Fire points must be weatherproofed, easily accessible, and conspicuously marked "FIRE POINT". Where water supply permits (sufficient pressure for a 5-6m jet at 30 litres/min), standpipes with 30m hose reels must be provided. Where water supply is insufficient, alternative provision of 2x 9-litre water extinguishers or 500-litre water tanks is required. Each fire point must have a manual alarm device.

LPG MANAGEMENT

Many holiday units rely on LPG for heating and cooking. Storage and use must comply with LPGA codes of practice. Exposed gas cylinders must not be within the separation boundary of an adjoining unit. Metered supplies from common storage tanks have additional requirements. The interaction of LPG with the fire triangle (ignition source + fuel + oxygen) creates a heightened risk profile unique to holiday park environments.

VEGETATION & SITE MANAGEMENT

Grass and vegetation must be cut at frequent intervals to prevent fire spread. Open fires should be prohibited or strictly controlled. BBQ facilities should be restricted to purpose-designed areas, remote from units. Waste storage must use purpose-designed containers with regular removal. The space beneath caravans must be kept clear of combustible materials.

EMERGENCY ACCESS

Emergency vehicles must be able to reach within 50m (Scotland) or 90m (England Model Standards 2008) of any unit. Site roads must be adequate for fire appliance access. Road widths, turning circles, and height clearances must accommodate emergency vehicles. Gateways must be a minimum of 3.1m wide with 3.7m height clearance.

DETECTION & WARNING

Individual letting units require appropriate automatic fire detection. For smaller self-catering units, a Grade F1 system (long-life sealed battery) under BS 5839 Part 6 may be suitable. Larger premises or those with higher risk profiles may require BS 5839 Part 1 Category L2 systems. Sounders must achieve 75 dB(A) at the bed-head to rouse sleeping occupants. Site-wide alarm arrangements may be needed for larger parks.

GUEST INFORMATION

Fire action notices must be displayed in every letting unit and at key locations across the site. Notices must indicate: evacuation procedure, alarm location, fire assembly point, and emergency contact numbers. Site plans showing fire points, assembly points, and emergency routes should be prominently displayed. Guests should receive fire safety information at check-in — verbally, in writing, or via a welcome pack.

THE FRA PROCESS — WHAT MUST BE ASSESSED?

The Scottish Government's guidance and the Home Office guides both follow a structured five-step approach to fire risk assessment. While the language and chapter structure varies between nations, the core methodology is consistent:

  • Step 1 — Identify persons at risk: Staff, guests (including children, elderly, disabled visitors), contractors, visitors. Consider people sleeping, people unfamiliar with the premises, people with mobility/sensory impairments.
  • Step 2 — Identify fire hazards: The fire triangle — sources of ignition (electrical equipment, cooking, heating, smoking, deliberate fire-raising), sources of fuel (furnishings, LPG, flammable liquids, waste, structural materials), and sources of oxygen (natural ventilation, air conditioning, stored oxidisers, medical oxygen).
  • Step 3 — Evaluate the risk: Consider the likelihood of fire occurring and the potential consequences to persons. Evaluate whether existing fire safety measures (detection, warning, escape routes, firefighting equipment, structural protection, management arrangements) are adequate or need improvement.
  • Step 4 — Record, plan, instruct, train: Record significant findings. Prepare an emergency fire action plan. Provide information and instruction to staff. Deliver fire safety training. Establish maintenance and testing regimes.
  • Step 5 — Review: The FRA must be kept under regular review. It must be reviewed when there is reason to suspect it is no longer valid, when there has been a significant change to the premises or activities, or following a fire or near-miss incident.

RECORDING, REVIEW & ENFORCEMENT

Recording:

  • England (from October 2023): All findings must be recorded, for all regulated premises, regardless of size or employee count.
  • Scotland: Recording required where 5+ employees, or premises subject to licensing/registration, or an Alterations Notice is in force. Since most holiday parks are licensed, recording is effectively mandatory.
  • Wales: Follows England (RRO applies).
  • Northern Ireland: Follows Scotland's threshold approach.

Review Frequency: No fixed statutory interval is prescribed in any UK nation. However, the consensus across all guidance documents is that FRAs should be reviewed:

  • At least annually for sleeping accommodation premises
  • Following any significant change to the premises, occupancy, or activities
  • Following a fire, near-miss, or enforcement action
  • When the assessor believes the existing assessment is no longer valid

Competence of Assessors: Both the Scottish Government and SFRS recommend using assessors who are third-party certificated by a UKAS-accredited certification body or registered with a Professional Registration Scheme. In England, the BAFE SP205 scheme provides a recognised standard. There is no statutory requirement to use a registered assessor — dutyholders may assess their own premises if competent to do so — but the use of unqualified assessors has been identified as a significant concern across the sector.

Maintaining Records Test and maintenance records should be retained for at least three years. This includes fire alarm testing (weekly), emergency lighting testing (monthly/annually), extinguisher servicing (annual), fire drill records, and staff training records. PENALTIES FOR NON-COMPLIANCE

The consequences of failing to comply with fire safety legislation are severe and have been strengthened significantly in recent years.

OFFENCE SCOTLAND ENGLAND & WALES NORTHERN IRELAND

Failure to comply with enforcement notice Fine and/or imprisonment up to 2 years Unlimited fine and/or up to 2 years imprisonment Fine and/or imprisonment
Failure to record FRA findings (where required) Fine Unlimited fine (from Oct 2023: applies to ALL premises) Fine
Putting persons at risk of death/serious injury from fire Unlimited fine and/or imprisonment up to 2 years Unlimited fine and/or up to 2 years imprisonment Unlimited fine and/or imprisonment
Impersonating an inspector / obstruction Fine Unlimited fine (increased from £1,000 in Oct 2023) Fine
Personal Liability In England and Wales, where a fire safety offence is committed by a body corporate (such as a limited company) and is proved to have been committed with the consent or connivance of, or to be attributable to any neglect on the part of, any director, manager, secretary, or similar officer, that individual as well as the body corporate is guilty of the offence and liable to be proceeded against and punished accordingly. This means directors and managers can face personal criminal prosecution.
