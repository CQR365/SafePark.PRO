Leadership

Understanding what motivates your managers

Before structure and expectations land on the General Managers, it pays to understand what actually drives each of them. Take each GM to dinner, one to one and away from the park, and find out what really motivates them — not the polished answer, the real one.

Why it matters

People are driven by very different things: recognition, being trusted with autonomy, money, security, progression, pride in their park, a quieter life, simply being listened to. When you know what motivates someone, you can manage expectations honestly and frame change in terms they actually care about. The same message — “we’re tightening up how we recruit and manage people” — lands completely differently depending on what a manager values. Knowing the difference is how you get genuine buy-in rather than reluctant compliance.

How it connects to the goal

Motivated managers run better parks, and better-run parks move the goal. It’s also where HR earns its keep: what you learn feeds the two-way performance reviews, the retention programme and team satisfaction, and shapes how change is communicated. HR can help you structure the conversation and quietly capture what matters to each manager, then build it into how reviews, recognition and expectations work.

Worth asking over dinner

  • What does a good year look like for you?
  • What frustrates you most right now?
  • What would make you think about leaving — or stay for years?
  • What would you change if it were your call?
  • Where do you want to be in two years?

What to listen for

Whether they’re driven more by money, recognition, autonomy, security, progression, pride or balance — then adapt how you get buy-in to each person. Same goal, different doors in.

Plain English

What good HR actually is

HR has one job: to make the relationship between the business and its people work — lawfully, fairly, and in a way that helps the business succeed.

It has two faces: an administrative, protective one (contracts, right to work, payroll inputs, records, keeping the company legal), and the valuable one (getting the right people in, keeping the good ones, developing them, handling conflict, shaping how it feels to work here). Weak HR lives only in the first; good HR uses the first to enable the second. The spine is the employee lifecycle: attract, recruit, onboard, manage and develop, reward, retain, exit. Most people problems trace back to a weak link in it.

What makes HR good

  • Aligned to the goal: every HR activity is a line of sight to being the best, top-rated park business.
  • Proactive, not reactive: prevents the fire — clear contracts, good induction, early conversations.
  • Fair and consistent: the same situation handled the same way every time, which is what makes a decision defensible.
  • Keeps good people: hiring is the cost; retention is the return. Hence recruit-and-retain, and team satisfaction.
  • Supports managers: equips and backs the GMs; doesn’t do everything for them, and doesn’t leave them guessing.
  • Well-documented and trusted: clean records that protect the business, plus the confidentiality that means people will come to HR.
The trap. HR drifting to one of two poles: paperwork police, or everyone’s mate. Good HR is neither — it is a clear-eyed steward of the people-business relationship, the same way safety is a clear-eyed steward of risk.

Where HR fits, and how it scales

HR is a support function; it never sits in the line that delivers the product. Under ~15 people there is no HR role. At ~15–80 (Highland Holidays) there is one HR person reporting to a director, advising the line managers who do the day-to-day. At ~80–250, an HR manager plus an assistant. At 250+, an HR director on the board. The line that defuses the misunderstanding: an HR Director only sits on the board in large organisations. At our size, HR is a single support role that reports into the Director — senior and valued, but not a second director.

CIPD & ongoing CPD

Aligned to the CIPD Profession Map, with the post-holder holding or working toward Associate or Chartered membership. CPD is built in via the CIPD cycle (identify, plan, act, reflect, record), outputs-based — impact over hours — with at least three CPD records a year (Jan–Dec) and readiness for the CIPD annual sample audit. This is what evidences, to the Directors, that the role is staying current.

The goal everything works fromHighland Holidays’ goal is to be the best and top-rated holiday park business in the UK — measured through Hoseasons, Reevoo, AA and Google. This profile defines the role the business needs, going forward, as the benchmark against which the role is resourced, supported and reviewed. It is a definition of the role, not a commentary on the post-holder.

Role purpose

To lead the people side of Highland Holidays across the parks and head office, with one purpose: to help the business reach its goal by recruiting and retaining the right people, building team satisfaction, running fair and lawful HR processes, supporting General Managers, and keeping accurate records — working with Quest on employment law and the H&S adviser on health-linked matters, and reporting to the Director.

What good HR looks like here

  • Aligned to the goal — every activity is a line of sight to it.
  • Proactive, not just reactive — prevents problems as well as fixing them.
  • Fair and consistent across all parks.
  • Keeps good people — understands why people stay and leave, and acts on it.
  • Works with the parks, not over them — supports the line; doesn’t run it.
  • Current and forward-looking, and lawful and well-recorded.

Document 3

HR Monthly Return — all three parks (v0.2)

One shared return that lives on the HR shared drive. Each month a fresh copy is opened, the parks complete their sections, HR consolidates for the Directors, the Directors respond, and once initialled it’s filed. It’s the people-side twin of the safety monthly return.

Part A — Park returns (GMs to HR)

A section each for Ben Nevis, Oban and Tyndrum: headcount, starters, leavers with reason, vacancies, probation, and notes on performance / training / development / team satisfaction, absence, employee relations, welfare and accommodation, plus a “for HR” flag.

Part B — Consolidated return (HR to the Directors)

HR rolls all three parks up: headcount, recruitment pipeline (incl. the always-on advert), turnover, retention & team satisfaction, onboarding & performance, training, absence, a high-level confidential employee-relations summary, compliance & currency, and risks / decisions for the Directors.

Part C — Directors’ response (back to HR)

Decisions and approvals, actions back to HR with owner and deadline, and any items escalated or parked — then an HR action log that feeds the outcomes back to the parks. Once Part C is complete the Director initials it and HR files the final version.
